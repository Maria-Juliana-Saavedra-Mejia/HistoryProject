Caligula: The Man Behind the Myth

Power, Politics, and the Making of a Roman Emperor

Gaius Caesar ruled Rome for under four years (AD 37–41), yet his name is a byword for madness. Almost everything we know about him was written by senators and their circle, often generations later. This exhibition asks you to weigh the evidence yourself.

Was Caligula truly a mad and tyrannical emperor, or was his reputation shaped and exaggerated by political enemies and biased historical accounts?

The Road to the Throne

Gaius Julius Caesar Germanicus was born on 31 August AD 12 at Antium, the son of Germanicus, a hugely popular general and adopted son of the emperor Tiberius, and Agrippina the Elder, a granddaughter of Augustus. Through both parents he belonged to the Julio-Claudian dynasty, with a claim to the blood of Augustus himself [Suetonius, Gaius 1–8; Britannica].

As a child he accompanied his father on campaign along the Rhine, where soldiers reportedly gave him the nickname Caligula, "little boot," after the miniature military boots he wore [Suetonius, Gaius 9; Tacitus, Annals 1.41]. Germanicus died in Syria in AD 19, and the family's fortunes collapsed under Tiberius and his Praetorian prefect Sejanus.

Agrippina was exiled in AD 29 and died in AD 33. His brothers Nero and Drusus Caesar were also destroyed. Caligula survived by living with his great-grandmother Livia, then his grandmother Antonia, and from about AD 31 with Tiberius on Capri [Suetonius, Gaius 10–12; Barrett 1989]. Surviving in that court required silence and caution. When Tiberius died on 16 March AD 37, Caligula was the obvious heir.

A New Emperor: AD 37

With the support of Macro, the Praetorian prefect, Caligula was acclaimed emperor and the Senate quickly voted him the powers of the principate. He had Tiberius's will set aside in his favour; Tiberius had named him joint heir with his own young grandson, Tiberius Gemellus [Suetonius, Gaius 14; Cassius Dio 59.1]. Many Romans saw in him the son of the beloved Germanicus, and the contrast with the aged, withdrawn Tiberius was striking.

Our sources describe a popular opening. He is said to have recalled exiles, ended treason trials, paid the bequests in Tiberius's will, and given gifts to the people and the Guard [Suetonius, Gaius 15–16; Cassius Dio 59.2–6]. Cassius Dio and Suetonius also report that late in AD 37 he fell seriously ill, and some ancient writers connect his later harshness to that illness. Modern historians treat that explanation cautiously [Philo, Embassy 14–22; Winterling 2011].

Documented

He became emperor in March AD 37 with Senate approval and Guard support, a smooth transfer after Tiberius's long reign.

Reported by hostile authors

Great public rejoicing and generous reforms. These come from writers who later condemned him, which makes them notable.

The puzzle

Why would the same sources praise a promising start and then describe a monster? Historians ask whether the story was shaped to fit a pattern.

Power, Politics, and Controversy

Every emperor depended on the Senate's cooperation and the army's loyalty. Caligula's reign shows what happens when that balance breaks.

Documented

Relations with the Senate

Rome's constitution was a monarchy dressed in Republican forms. Senators resented that real power lay with the emperor. Conspiracy cases in AD 39 (involving Lepidus and Gaetulicus) and later executions of senators are broadly accepted, though ancient numbers and motives are less certain [Cassius Dio 59.22; Suetonius, Gaius 24].

Documented

Building and spending

Works begun under him include two major aqueducts (the Aqua Claudia and Anio Novus, later completed by Claudius) and a circus on the Vatican plain. Dio claims he drained the treasury through lavish games and gifts [Suetonius, Gaius 21, 37–38; Cassius Dio 59.2, 59.28].

Documented

Provinces and crises

Riots between Greeks and Jews in Alexandria (AD 38) and a dispute over placing imperial statues in Jerusalem (AD 40) were handled in his reign. Philo and Josephus, both writing from the Jewish perspective, give contemporary or near-contemporary accounts [Philo, Embassy to Gaius; Josephus, Antiquities 18–19].

Disputed

Divine honours

Ancient authors say he demanded worship as a living god. Roman rulers were honoured in the provinces and deified after death. Whether Caligula's claims were unusual or exaggerated by hostile writers is debated [Suetonius, Gaius 22; Cassius Dio 59.26–28].

Disputed

The Baiae bridge and the northern campaign

Suetonius and Dio describe a bridge of boats across the Bay of Naples and a strange campaign on the Channel coast in which soldiers collected shells. Josephus gives a different motive, and modern scholars offer rational explanations, from military display to a failed or aborted invasion [Suetonius, Gaius 19, 46; Cassius Dio 59.17, 59.25].

Uncertain

Reading the evidence

Much of what follows in the investigation below sits between these categories: reported in more than one source, but impossible to confirm independently.

Why source criticism matters

Ancient authors were not neutral reporters. Suetonius (writing about AD 121) and Cassius Dio (writing in the early 3rd century) wrote long after Caligula's death, drawing on earlier accounts that are now lost. Many came from the senatorial class, which had reason to condemn an emperor who humiliated and executed senators. Tacitus's account of Caligula's reign has not survived. Ask of each source: Who wrote this? When? For whom? What did they gain by writing it this way?

The Fall of an Emperor: AD 41

By AD 41 Caligula had alienated senators, and ancient sources say also members of the Praetorian Guard. Some conspirators were driven by personal grievance, others by fear. Cassius Chaerea, a tribune of the Guard, is said to have been mocked by the emperor, and Cornelius Sabinus joined him [Josephus, Antiquities 19.17–27; Suetonius, Gaius 56–58].

On 24 January AD 41, during the Palatine Games, Caligula was cut down in a covered passage near the theatre. His wife Caesonia and their infant daughter Julia Drusilla were killed as well. The conspirators may have hoped to restore senatorial government, but the Praetorians had other ideas [Josephus, Antiquities 19.99–113; Cassius Dio 59.29–30].

    The Legacy: How Should History Remember Caligula?

    Caligula became infamous partly because the surviving narratives were written by his critics, and partly because lurid stories travel well. Anecdotes about excess and cruelty became a template for the "bad emperor" in later literature, and the tradition shaped how later generations, and modern film, imagined him.

    Historians draw a useful line between what he did (decisions, laws, buildings, foreign policy, executions) and stories told about him (private behaviour, jokes, rumours). Barrett and Winterling, among others, argue that his reign makes sense as a struggle over power between an emperor and the aristocracy, rather than as simple insanity. Others still see real instability. The evidence does not allow certainty [Barrett 1989; Winterling 2011; Wilkinson 2005].

    His reign shows how much Roman imperial power depended on personal relationships, and how fragile the system was when an emperor lost the elite's trust. The Praetorians' choice of Claudius in AD 41 set a precedent that later emperors would exploit.
